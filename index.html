Lecture 1

Organization of the Human Body

The opening lecture is the vocabulary of the course: how the body is organized, the four tissue types, connective tissue and its matrix, and the four groups of organic molecules. Expect straightforward recall questions here, so learn the lists and pairs well.

1. Ways of studying the body

  • Anatomy is the study of body structure, classically by dissection.
  • Physiology is the study of body function, meaning how those structures work.
  • Pathophysiology (pathology) is physiology gone wrong, the study of disease. Normal function is physiology; diseased function is pathophysiology.

You need structure to understand function. That is why the course opens with anatomy.

2. Levels of organization and division of labor

Atom → Molecule → Macromolecule → Organelle → Cell → Tissue → Organ → System → Organism

The slide version shortens this to six levels: Chemical → Cellular → Tissue → Organ → System → Organismic. Example from the figure: DNA (chemical) → smooth muscle cell (cellular) → smooth muscle tissue → stomach (organ) → digestive system → child (organism).

Division of labor means the work of keeping the organism alive is divided among different parts. Each part has a specific job and works in harmony with the others, and the job of the smallest unit builds up to the next level.

The cell is a physical system made of atoms and molecules, and it is a dynamic structure that is constantly changing.

4. Tissues: four primary types

A tissue is a group of cells working together to perform a specific function.

TissueJobExamples
EpithelialCovers surfaces, lines cavities, forms glands. Protection, absorption, secretion.Epidermis, lining of the GI tract and hollow organs
ConnectiveSupport: binds, supports, protects. Most diverse and most abundant.Bone, cartilage, blood, connective tissue proper (fat, tendon)

Epithelium is named by layers + shape of the apical (top) cells

  • Simple = one layer. Found where absorption and filtration happen (blood vessel lining, lung alveoli).
  • Stratified = several layers. Found where protection from wear is needed (skin, mouth).
  • Pseudostratified = one layer that looks layered because the nuclei sit at different heights. Often ciliated (respiratory tract).
  • Shapes: squamous (flat), cuboidal (cube), columnar (tall).
Trap: pseudostratified epithelium is a single layer. The name is defined by the shape of the apical layer of cells.

5. Connective tissue = cells + extracellular matrix (ECM)

  • The ECM is the non-cellular part, secreted by the cells: a gel-like ground substance plus fibers (collagen for strength, elastin for stretch, and reticular fibers). ECM is one component of connective tissue, not a synonym for it.
  • Connective tissue cells are scattered within the ECM rather than packed tightly like epithelium.
  • Different connective tissues have different combinations of ECM fibers, which is what gives each one its properties.

Origin: mesenchyme

All connective tissue comes from mesenchyme, an embryonic tissue full of stem cells. It can become any connective tissue cell type depending on the growth factors and signalling it receives (its "plastic potential").

Mesenchyme givesMature cellClass of connective tissue
FibroblastFibrocyteConnective tissue proper (skin dermis, tendons, ligaments)
ChondroblastChondrocyteCartilage
OsteoblastOsteocyteBone (osseous tissue)
Hematopoietic stem cellBlood cells (and macrophages)Blood

"-blast" cells build and secrete matrix. "-cyte" cells are the mature cells that maintain it.

  • Blood is a "fluid connective tissue": RBCs and WBCs in a liquid ECM, the plasma. Plasma proteins are made mainly by the liver (hepatocytes). Blood transports respiratory gases, nutrients and wastes, and stays inside blood vessels.
  • Connective tissue proper (areolar, loose): a gel-like matrix with all three fiber types. Its cells are fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells and some WBCs. It wraps and cushions organs, its macrophages eat bacteria, it plays a role in inflammation, and it holds and conveys tissue fluid. It lies under epithelia (lamina propria of mucous membranes) and surrounds capillaries. Fixed cells include adipocytes and mesenchymal (repair) cells.
  • Bone: Haversian (central) canals are microscopic tubes in cortical (compact) bone. They carry blood vessels and nerves that supply O₂ and nutrients to the osteocytes. One osteon = a Haversian canal plus its concentric rings of matrix. Cancellous (spongy) bone sits inside; the periosteum covers the outside.

6. The four groups of organic molecules

Carbohydrates · Lipids · Proteins · Nucleic acids

Lipids

  • Defining feature: they don't dissolve readily in water (hydrophobic). Made of C, H and O, with much more H than carbohydrates.
  • Phospholipids have a glycerol + phosphate hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. In water they form a bilayer, the main component of cell membranes.
  • Glycerides are the most common type of lipid: glycerol (an alcohol) + one or more fatty acids. The bond forms between the fatty acid's COOH group and glycerol's OH. Triglycerides (3 fatty acids) are the most common dietary lipid. Fat is broken into glycerol + fatty acids to be used for energy and combined into triglycerides for storage.
  • Saturated fatty acids have no C=C double bonds, pack straight, are solid at room temperature (fats, butter), and are linked to heart disease. Unsaturated fatty acids have double bonds that put a kink in the chain, so they are liquid (oils). Examples: palmitic acid C16:0 is saturated; oleic and linoleic acid are unsaturated (linoleic is polyunsaturated).
  • Steroids are the second class of lipids: four fused carbon rings plus side chains, structurally very different from triglycerides. Cholesterol is part of cell membranes and the precursor of other steroids (testosterone, estrogen, progesterone).

Carbohydrates

  • Contain C, H and O (usually nothing else). Building blocks are monosaccharides (simple sugars). Glucose is our most important energy source.
  • Polysaccharides (complex carbohydrates):
    • Starch: plant storage form (seeds, roots).
    • Glycogen: animal storage form, stored in liver and muscle.
    • Cellulose: most abundant carbohydrate on Earth. Rigid plant support. Humans can't digest it, but it is important as fiber that moves food through the gut.

Proteins

  • Proteins are chains of amino acids. There are 20 amino acids, and each has an amino group, a carboxyl group and a side chain (R).
  • A chain of 10–30 amino acids is a polypeptide. A longer chain that folds is a protein.
  • The order of amino acids determines the 3D shape, and shape determines function.
  • Four levels of structure: primary (sequence), secondary (backbone H-bonding gives α-helix and β-pleated sheet), tertiary (3D folding from side-chain interactions), quaternary (more than one chain).
  • Protein jobs from the slide table: motility (actin, myosin, tubulin), structural (collagen, keratin), enzymes (catalase, DNA polymerase), transport (hemoglobin, porin), hormones (insulin, FSH), cell-surface receptors, neurotransmitters (endorphins), immunoglobulins (antibodies), toxins (ricin, snake venom).
  • Lipoproteins are lipid + protein particles that transport fat. HDL ("good") carries cholesterol to the liver for removal. LDL ("bad") carries cholesterol to the arteries.
  • Glycoproteins are protein + carbohydrate, usually on the cell surface, where they act as receptors for outside signals.

Nucleic acids

  • Chains of nucleotides. Each nucleotide = phosphate + sugar + nitrogenous base.
  • DNA: sugar is deoxyribose, bases are A, T, G, C. Human cell DNA is about 3 billion nucleotides. The nucleotide order encodes the amino acid order of proteins.
  • RNA: sugar is ribose, uracil replaces thymine. RNA is the intermediate between DNA and protein.
  • Purines (two rings) are adenine and guanine. Pyrimidines (one ring) are thymine and cytosine (uracil in RNA). Base pairs: A–T and G–C, held by hydrogen bonds.
  • Central dogma: DNA →(replication) DNA; DNA →(transcription) RNA →(translation) protein; RNA →(reverse transcription) DNA.

Lecture 2

Membrane Potential, Neurotransmission & the NMJ

This lecture follows one signal from start to finish: a resting neuron → an action potential → transmitter release at a synapse → the neuromuscular junction → calcium release inside the muscle fiber. If you can tell that story in order, and say what goes wrong when each step is blocked by a drug, you have the lecture.

Resting potential → Action potential → Neurotransmission → End-plate potential → Muscle AP → T-tubule → DHP → RyR → Ca²⁺ → contraction

1. Who sends the signal: somatic vs autonomic motor neurons

Somatic motor neuronsAutonomic motor neurons
ControlVoluntaryInvoluntary
TargetSkeletal muscleInternal organs, glands, cardiac and smooth muscle
Transmitter at targetAcetylcholine (ACh)Sympathetic ("fight or flight"): norepinephrine. Parasympathetic ("rest and digest"): ACh

Neuron shapes in the slide: multipolar interneurons, motor neurons (dendrites → cell body → axon hillock → myelinated axon with nodes of Ranvier → axon terminals on muscle), and sensory neurons (receptor cell → peripheral branch → cell body → central branch).

2. Resting membrane potential (RMP)

At rest, nerve and muscle cells are polarized: the inside is negative, about −70 to −90 mV (the AP graph uses −90 mV for large fibers).

IonInside (mM)Outside (mM)
K⁺1404
Na⁺12150
Cl⁻4120
A⁻ (proteins etc.)14834

Two things build the RMP:

  1. Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in using ATP. This creates and maintains the concentration gradients.
  2. Selective permeability: at rest the membrane is much more permeable to K⁺ (leak K⁺ channels are always open). K⁺ leaks out down its gradient, carrying positive charge out, which leaves the inside negative. Few Na⁺ or Cl⁻ channels are open at rest.

3. The action potential (AP)

  1. A stimulus opens some Na⁺ channels, giving a local, graded depolarization.
  2. If it reaches threshold, it becomes all-or-nothing: masses of voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open, Na⁺ rushes in, and the inside turns positive (depolarization, overshoot to about +35 mV).
  3. Na⁺ channels inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels open. K⁺ rushes out (repolarization).
  4. The K⁺ channels stay open a little too long, so the membrane dips below rest (hyperpolarization).

How the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel works

  • Resting: the activation gate is closed. Its voltage-sensing α-helix carries positive charges.
  • Opening: depolarization moves the voltage-sensing helices and the channel opens in less than 0.1 ms. Na⁺ enters.
  • Inactivation: within about 0.5–1.0 ms a channel-inactivating segment plugs the pore. The channel can't reopen yet: this is the refractory period.
  • Reset: only after the membrane repolarizes (slow, several ms) does the gate close and the inactivating segment move away, ready for the next AP.

Four kinds of ion channel

ChannelOpens when…
Resting K⁺ (leak)Always open. Sets the RMP.
Voltage-gatedMembrane potential changes (opens transiently). Example: Na⁺ for the AP, Ca²⁺ at the presynaptic terminal.
Ligand-gatedA specific extracellular neurotransmitter binds. Example: the nicotinic ACh receptor on the postsynaptic membrane.
Signal-gatedA specific intracellular molecule binds.

Location rule: the presynaptic terminal has voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels. The postsynaptic membrane has ligand-gated (transmitter-gated) channels. Having different channels on different sides allows fine-tuning of the message.

4. Myelin and saltatory conduction

  • Myelin is a specialized membrane of lipids and proteins. It is made by Schwann cells in the PNS and oligodendrocytes in the CNS, both of which are glial cells.
  • Nodes of Ranvier are the bare gaps between myelin segments and are packed with voltage-gated Na⁺ channels.
  • Saltatory conduction: Na⁺ influx at one node depolarizes that spot. Positive charge flows quickly down the axon because myelin stops it leaking out, and it depolarizes the next node, which fires its own AP. The AP "jumps" node to node. This is faster and uses less energy than continuous conduction in unmyelinated axons.

6. Chemical transmission step by step

  1. Transmitter is synthesized and stored in vesicles.
  2. An AP invades the presynaptic terminal.
  3. Depolarization opens voltage-gated Ca²⁺ channels.
  4. Ca²⁺ flows in.
  5. Ca²⁺ binds synaptotagmin (the Ca²⁺ sensor). It changes shape and engages the SNARE complex, and the vesicle fuses with the membrane.
  6. Transmitter is released by exocytosis at the active zone.
  7. Transmitter binds receptors on the postsynaptic membrane.
  8. Postsynaptic channels open or close, changing excitability (excitatory or inhibitory current).
  9. The signal is ended by removal of transmitter: enzymatic breakdown, reuptake, or uptake by glial cells.
  10. Vesicle membrane is retrieved by endocytosis and recycled.

Vesicle cycle: import transmitter → move to active zone → dock → Ca²⁺-triggered exocytosis → clathrin-coated endocytosis → refill.

Without Ca²⁺, synaptotagmin and the SNAREs can't bind properly. Ca²⁺ changes synaptotagmin's shape so it can bind the SNAREs and stabilize the complex.

MAP2 (microtubule-associated protein 2) is the neuronal scaffold. It binds and stabilizes microtubules, is abundant in dendrites, supports dendritic branching and spines, and keeps transport "tracks" intact. It is used as a dendrite marker in the hippocampal neuron image.

7. The neuromuscular junction (NMJ)

  • Skeletal muscle fibers are innervated by large, myelinated nerve fibers from large motor neurons in the anterior horns of the spinal cord (lower motor neurons). Upper motor neurons in the brain synapse on them.
  • Each nerve ending makes one junction with a fiber, the NMJ, near the fiber's midpoint.
  • Motor end plate: the axon terminal sits in a synaptic trough (gutter) of the muscle membrane. The membrane under it is folded into subneural clefts, which increase the surface area for receptors. Teloglial (Schwann) cells cover the terminal. The terminal is packed with mitochondria (for acetyl-CoA and ATP) and synaptic vesicles.

ACh: synthesis, storage, release, breakdown

  1. Synthesis in the terminal cytoplasm: acetyl-CoA + choline → ACh, catalyzed by choline acetyltransferase. Acetyl-CoA comes from the many mitochondria in the terminal.
  2. Storage in synaptic vesicles, along with ATP and peptides.
  3. Release depends on extracellular Ca²⁺: the AP opens Ca²⁺ channels, and the rise in intracellular Ca²⁺ destabilizes the vesicles so they release ACh.
  4. Breakdown: acetylcholinesterase (AChE) in the cleft rapidly splits ACh into acetate + choline.
  5. Recycling: choline is transported back into the terminal to make more ACh.

End-plate potential (EPP)

  • ACh binds nicotinic ACh receptors, which are ligand-gated cation channels. Mostly Na⁺ pours in (a little K⁺ leaves). Negative charges at the channel mouth keep Cl⁻ and other anions out.
  • The result is a local positive potential change, the EPP. It is a graded potential.
  • A sudden rise of more than 20–30 mV is normally enough to open more and more voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, which fires a muscle action potential.
  • Safety factor: each nerve impulse produces about 3× the EPP needed to excite the fiber.

8. Excitation–contraction coupling

  1. The muscle AP spreads along the sarcolemma and dives into the fiber through transverse (T) tubules.
  2. The T-tubule membrane holds dihydropyridine (DHP) receptors, which are voltage sensors. The AP changes their shape.
  3. In skeletal muscle the DHP receptor is mechanically linked to the ryanodine receptor (RyR), the Ca²⁺ release channel in the terminal cisternae of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR). The DHP receptor "tugs" the RyR open.
  4. Ca²⁺ floods from the SR into the sarcoplasm and starts contraction. One T-tubule plus the two cisternae on either side is called a triad.
  5. Relaxation: on repolarization the DHP receptor closes the release channels, and an ATP-dependent Ca²⁺ pump (SERCA) actively pumps Ca²⁺ back into the SR. Inside the SR, Ca²⁺ is held by calsequestrin.
Trap: the immediate trigger for SR Ca²⁺ release in skeletal muscle is the voltage change sensed by DHP receptors, not Ca²⁺ entering from outside. (Cardiac muscle is the one that needs extracellular Ca²⁺.) ATP is needed for relaxation, to pump Ca²⁺ back.

9. Ending the signal and modifying the EPP: drugs and toxins

To fire a muscle AP, the EPP must reach threshold. Anything that shrinks the EPP below threshold causes weakness or paralysis. Anything that prolongs ACh action causes spasm.

AgentMechanismEffect
Curare (tubocurarine)Competitive antagonist: occupies the nicotinic ACh receptor without opening itWeak EPP, below threshold → paralysis (curve A)

10. Fatigue of the junction

Stimulating the nerve at more than 100 times per second for several minutes uses up ACh vesicles faster than they can be refilled. The EPP shrinks below threshold and impulses fail to pass. This is the same mechanism as synaptic fatigue in the CNS.

Lecture 3

Mechanism of Muscle Contraction

Lecture 2 got calcium into the sarcoplasm. This lecture explains what calcium does: it uncovers actin so myosin heads can grab and pull. Then it covers what powers the pulling (ATP and how it is regenerated), how fast and slow fibers differ, and how muscle remodels itself. The cross-bridge cycle and the energy systems are the high-yield parts.

1. Anatomy, from whole muscle to filament

Muscle → Fasciculus (bundle) → Muscle fiber (one cell) → Myofibrils (hundreds to thousands per fiber) → Actin and myosin filaments
  • About 40% of the body is skeletal muscle.
  • Each myofibril has about 1500 myosin filaments and 3000 actin filaments lying side by side. These are responsible for contraction.
  • Sarcolemma = a true plasma membrane plus an outer coat of polysaccharide containing thin collagen fibrils. At each end of the fiber it fuses with a tendon fiber. Tendon fibers bundle into tendons that insert into bone.
  • Sarcoplasm = the intracellular fluid between myofibrils.
  • Sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) = the specialized endoplasmic reticulum of muscle that stores Ca²⁺.

2. The sarcomere and its bands

StructureWhat it is
I band (light)Actin only. Isotropic to polarized light
A band (dark)Myosin plus the overlapping ends of actin. Anisotropic to polarized light
H zoneCenter of the A band where there is myosin only (no actin overlap)
M lineCenter of the sarcomere where myosin filaments are linked

SarcomereSegment between two successive Z discs. About 2 µm when contracted
TitinGiant springy protein running from the Z disc to the M line. It holds myosin and actin in register (framework), stores elastic energy when stretched, recoils, and resists over-stretch
Cross-bridgesMyosin heads sticking out of the thick filament. Their interaction with actin causes contraction

Why 2 µm matters: at about 2.0–2.2 µm the actin completely overlaps the myosin cross-bridges and the actin tips just begin to overlap each other. Every cross-bridge can bind, so this is the length of greatest force.

3. The two filaments

  • Myosin (thick filament) is made of many myosin molecules. Each molecule has a head, tail and hinge (the slide also names light meromyosin as the tail and heavy meromyosin as the head plus arm). Each head has an actin-binding site and an ATPase site.
  • Actin (thin filament) is made of actin, tropomyosin and troponin. F-actin is a double-stranded helix of G-actin with active sites. Tropomyosin lies over the active sites at rest. The troponin complex binds Ca²⁺.

4. General sequence (memorize the 8 steps)

  1. An AP travels along a motor nerve to the motor end plate.
  2. The nerve secretes ACh.
  3. ACh binds the sarcolemma and opens gated channels.
  4. Large amounts of Na⁺ enter and start an AP.
  5. The AP travels along the sarcolemma, just as in a nerve.
  6. The AP depolarizes the membrane and triggers Ca²⁺ release from the SR.
  7. Ca²⁺ starts the contraction cycle.
  8. After contraction, Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR.

5. Sliding filament theory and the cross-bridge cycle

Filaments do not shorten. Actin slides over myosin toward the center and pulls the Z discs closer, so the sarcomere shortens. The force comes from the interaction of myosin cross-bridges with actin ("walk-along" mechanism: heads bend back and forth and step along the actin).

Resting state: the myosin head has already split ATP (ATPase activity) but keeps the products, ADP + Pi, bound. It is "cocked", perpendicular and energized.

  1. Active-site exposure: Ca²⁺ binds troponin, and the troponin–tropomyosin complex shifts to uncover actin's active sites.
  2. Cross-bridge formation: the cocked myosin head binds the exposed site.
  3. Power stroke (pivoting): binding causes a conformational change. The head tilts toward the arm and pulls actin toward the M line. ADP + Pi are released. This is where force is generated.
  4. Detachment: a new ATP binds the head, which then releases actin. (Without ATP the head stays locked on actin. This is the rigor state, as in rigor mortis.)
  5. Reactivation: the head hydrolyzes ATP to ADP + Pi, which re-cocks it to perpendicular, ready for the next stroke.

The cycle repeats as long as Ca²⁺ is high and ATP is available. Relaxation happens when Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR (also ATP-dependent): tropomyosin covers the sites again.

Traps: ATP binding causes detachment; ATP hydrolysis re-cocks the head; release of ADP + Pi goes with the power stroke. Ca²⁺ binds troponin, not tropomyosin and not myosin. The A band does not change length during contraction.

6. Length–tension and load–velocity

  • Over-stretched (D): little actin–myosin overlap, few cross-bridges, low force.
  • Over-shortened (A): actin filaments overlap each other and Z discs bump into myosin ends, so fewer effective cross-bridges.
  • Load vs velocity: with no load, a muscle contracts fastest and reaches full contraction in about 0.1 s. As the load increases, velocity falls progressively and reaches zero when the load equals the maximum force the muscle can generate.

7. Energetics: where the ATP comes from

  • Contracting against a load does work = load × distance. The energy comes from chemical reactions in the muscle.
  • Efficiency is under 25%. The rest becomes heat.
  • ATP powers both the cross-bridge cycle and the Ca²⁺ pump.

Summary table from the slide: 0–2 min is dominated by ATP-PCr (immediate, very fast, short-term); 2 min to 1–2 hr by glucose (glycolysis + oxidative); more than 2 hr by fat oxidation.

Glycolysis details

  • Glucose (6C) → glucose-6-phosphate → fructose-6-phosphate → fructose-1,6-bisphosphate → 2 × glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (3C) → 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate → 3-phosphoglycerate → phosphoenolpyruvate → 2 pyruvate.
  • Energy-investment phase spends 2 ATP (like a loan), the harvesting phase makes 4 ATP + 2 NADH. Net gain: 2 ATP.
  • Glycolysis and the Krebs cycle make ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation. The ETC makes most ATP by oxidative phosphorylation. NADH and FADH₂ carry electrons to the ETC.
  • Krebs intermediates (carbons): citrate C6 → α-ketoglutarate C5 → succinate C4 → fumarate C4 → oxaloacetate C4. CO₂ is released as waste.
  • Pyruvate's fate: no O₂ → lactate (or ethanol in yeast), only 2 ATP. With O₂ → mitochondria, many more ATP.

Cori cycle (gluconeogenesis from lactate)

Working muscle does anaerobic glycolysis (glucose → pyruvate → lactate, +2 ATP). Lactate travels in the blood to the liver, which turns it back into glucose (gluconeogenesis, which costs 6 ATP). The glucose returns to the muscle.

8. Characteristics of contraction

  • Motor unit = one motor nerve fiber plus all the muscle fibers it innervates.
  • Isometric contraction: muscle length stays the same, tension changes (holding posture, holding your head up). Isotonic contraction: tension stays the same, length changes (lifting a weight).
  • Duration of an isometric twitch varies by muscle: ocular muscle is fastest (shortest), gastrocnemius intermediate, soleus slowest (about 200 ms). There is a short latent period between the AP and the start of contraction.

10. Remodelling: hypertrophy and atrophy

  • Muscles are continually remodelled to match their function: diameter, length, strength and vascular supply all change. The process is rapid, within a few weeks.
  • Hypertrophy = increase in total muscle mass. It comes from more actin and myosin (more myofibrils) enlarging existing fibers, not new fibers. It is much greater when the muscle is loaded during contraction. A few strong contractions a day give significant hypertrophy in 6–10 weeks. Energy enzymes (e.g. glycolytic) also increase.
  • Training slide: strength = 1–5 reps with 3–5 min rest; hypertrophy = 6–12 reps with 1–2 min rest.
  • Atrophy = decrease in mass. With weeks of disuse (cast, bed rest, spaceflight), protein degradation outpaces replacement. The pathway is the ATP-dependent ubiquitin–proteasome pathway: ubiquitin tags proteins for destruction, and the proteasome (a large protein complex) degrades them by proteolysis (breaking peptide bonds).

Lecture 4

Hemodynamics I

Hemodynamics is the physics of blood flow. This first lecture is the map: the two circulations, the path through the heart, the vessel types and what each is for, where the blood volume sits, and how pressure falls along the way. Learn the numbers in this lecture; they come back in Hemodynamics II and Microcirculation.

1. Two circulations

Pulmonary circulation (gas exchange loop)Systemic circulation (delivery loop)
PathRight ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atriumLeft ventricle → aorta → arteries → tissues → veins → venae cavae → right atrium
JobUnload CO₂, load O₂Deliver O₂ and nutrients to every organ (including the brain); pick up CO₂ and wastes
PressureLow (pulmonary artery about 25/8 mmHg)High (aorta about 120/80, mean about 100 mmHg)

Pulmonary circulation is the closed circuit shuttling blood between the lungs and the heart. In the pulmonary capillary, O₂ is loaded from alveolar air into blood and CO₂ is unloaded from blood into alveolar air.

2. Path through the heart (two pumps in one)

Body → Superior vena cava (head, arms) + Inferior vena cava (lower body) → Right atrium → Tricuspid valve → Right ventricle → Pulmonary valve → Pulmonary artery → Lungs → Pulmonary veins → Left atrium → Mitral (bicuspid) valve → Left ventricle → Aortic valve → Aorta → Body
  • The inferior vena cava is the largest vein. It returns deoxygenated, CO₂-carrying blood from the lower body to the right atrium.
  • The aorta is the largest artery. The ascending aorta leaves the heart, arches in an inverted "U" and descends.
  • Pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood (away from the heart). Pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood (back to the heart). Arteries and veins are named by direction, not by oxygen content.

6. Pressure falls along the circuit

  • Aorta: mean about 100 mmHg. Because pumping is pulsatile it swings between 120 (systolic) and 80 (diastolic).
  • Systemic capillaries: about 35 mmHg at the arteriolar end, about 10 mmHg at the venous end, and an average "functional" pressure of about 17 mmHg. This is enough to push nutrients out through the pores, low enough not to damage the thin walls, and low enough to let fluid return at the venous end.
  • Venae cavae / right atrium: near 0. Blood has lost its energy over a long distance with lots of friction, and stretchy veins lower the pressure further.
  • Pulmonary artery: much lower than the aorta, about 25/8 mmHg. Three reasons: (1) function: it only needs to expose blood to alveolar gas; (2) shorter distance and lower resistance (more compliant vessels); (3) structure: thinner, less muscular pulmonary arteries. The low pressure also protects the delicate alveoli from fluid leakage and damage.

7. Preview: forces moving fluid across the capillary wall

Two main forces (developed fully in Microcirculation):

  • Capillary hydrostatic pressure (CHP) is blood pressure pushing fluid out. Highest at the arterial end, lowest at the venous end.
  • Blood colloid osmotic (oncotic) pressure (BCOP) comes from plasma proteins (mainly albumin) that can't leave. It pulls fluid in and stays roughly constant along the capillary (about 25 mmHg in this figure).

NFP = CHP − BCOP. Positive means filtration (out); negative means reabsorption (in); zero means no net movement.

Lecture 5

Hemodynamics II

This lecture is about three quantities and how they relate: pressure difference, flow and resistance. Then it covers what sets resistance (vessel radius, viscosity and length) and how the body adjusts flow (autoregulation and sympathetic tone). Expect calculation-style MCQs built on the r⁴ rule.

Garden hose analogy: the tap pressure is the heart, the nozzle opening is the vessel resistance, and the stream is blood flow. A tightly squeezed nozzle (high resistance) gives a small stream; a wide-open nozzle (low resistance) gives a large one.

1. Blood pressure

  • Blood pressure is the force exerted by the blood against any unit area of the vessel wall. It is not the blood itself.
  • It is measured in mmHg because the mercury manometer has been the standard reference since its invention in 1846 by Poiseuille.
  • 50 mmHg means the force is enough to push a column of mercury 50 mm high against gravity. A reading of 120/80 means the peak pushes mercury 120 mm and the lowest point 80 mm.

2. Flow = ΔP / R (Ohm's law for blood)

F = ΔP / R   ⇔   ΔP = F × R   ⇔   R = ΔP / F
  • Flow depends on the pressure difference (gradient) between the two ends, not on absolute pressure. Blood flows from high to low pressure.
  • Flow is directly proportional to ΔP. Slide example: vessel 1 has 50 → 10 mmHg (ΔP = 40); vessel 2 has 90 → 10 (ΔP = 80). With the same resistance, vessel 2 has twice the flow.
  • Flow is inversely proportional to R. Clogged arteries (high R) reduce flow. Shock (low ΔP) reduces flow.
  • In the systemic circulation the driving gradient runs from the aorta (highest pressure) to the great veins (lowest).

3. Poiseuille's law: what sets resistance

F = π ΔP r⁴ / 8 η l   so   R = 8 η l / π r⁴

r = radius, η = viscosity, l = vessel length.

  • Radius (to the 4th power) is the dominant factor. R ∝ 1/r⁴, so flow ∝ r⁴.
    • Diameters 1 : 2 : 4 give flows of 1 : 16 : 256 ml/min at the same 100 mmHg (Figure 14-8).
    • Double the radius → resistance falls to 1/16 → flow ×16.
    • Halve the radius → resistance ×16 → flow falls to 1/16.
  • Viscosity: more viscous means more resistance and less flow.
  • Length: longer means more resistance and less flow.
  • This is why arterioles control flow: small changes in their diameter cause huge changes in resistance, which redirects blood (to muscle in exercise, to skin for cooling).
  • Inside a vessel, flow is laminar with concentric rings. The farther from the wall, the faster (fastest in the center), because friction is greatest at the wall.
Worked example. An arteriole constricts so its radius falls by half. Pressure is unchanged. New resistance = 2⁴ = 16× higher. New flow = 1/16 of before (about 6%).
Another. Radius rises by 20% (1.2×). Flow = 1.2⁴ ≈ 2.07×, so roughly double.

4. Viscosity and hematocrit

  • Viscosity is the "thickness" or internal friction of a fluid. Whole blood is about 3–4× as viscous as water. Plasma is only about 1.5×.
  • Hematocrit = the proportion of blood volume that is RBCs. A hematocrit of 40 means 40% cells and 60% plasma. Hematocrit is the main determinant of blood viscosity.
  • Viscosity rises steeply (non-linearly) as hematocrit increases (Figure 14-11).
StateHematocritViscosity / resistanceConsequence
NormalAbout 40–45%OptimalBalance of O₂ carrying and easy flow
AnemiaLow (e.g. 15–20%)Lower: blood is "thinner", easier to pumpLess O₂ carried despite easier flow
Polycythemia (or dehydration)High (e.g. 60–70%)Much higher: "sticky" bloodHeart works harder, flow can fall

Physiology tie-in from the notes: living at high altitude (low O₂) stimulates RBC production, which raises hematocrit.

5. Autoregulation

  • Vascular autoregulation is the ability of the blood vessels of the brain, heart and kidney to change their tone so that flow stays relatively constant despite changes in arterial pressure.
  • Figure 14-12: between about 70 and 175 mmHg mean arterial pressure, tissue flow is almost flat. When pressure rises, vessels constrict (R rises), so F = ΔP/R barely changes.
  • Sympathetic stimulation or vasoconstrictor hormones (norepinephrine, angiotensin II, vasopressin, endothelin) shift the curve down. Reduced flow is rarely maintained for more than a few hours, because local autoregulation brings flow back toward normal.

6. Pressure–flow in a passive vessel and sympathetic tone

  • In a passive vessel, more pressure gives more flow. The curve bends upward because higher pressure stretches the vessel open, lowering R.
  • Critical closing pressure is the pressure below which the vessel collapses and flow stops. Smooth-muscle tone squeezes the vessel; if the internal pressure falls below that squeeze, it pinches shut (like a squeezed straw).
  • Sympathetic stimulation: norepinephrine causes vasoconstriction. The curve shifts down and to the right: less flow at any pressure, and a higher critical closing pressure. Example: during heavy exercise, gut and kidney vessels constrict and blood is redirected to muscle.
  • Sympathetic inhibition: vasodilation. The curve shifts up and to the left: more flow at any pressure, and a lower critical closing pressure. Example: skin vessels dilate to dump heat (flushed skin).

Optional · 2025 notes

Vascular Distensibility

Check whether this was covered. This section comes from last year's student notes (Lectures 6–7, "Vascular Distensibility"). No 2026 slide deck for it was uploaded. If your professor taught it this year, it is fair game; if not, skip it. Its practice questions are tagged so you can include or exclude them.

Vessels are stretchy, and how stretchy each type is explains why veins hold most of the blood, why we have a pulse pressure, why blood pressure rises with age, and why legs swell when you stand still.

1. Why distensibility matters

  • The heart pumps in spurts. If vessels were rigid pipes, flow would be jerky. Because all vessels are distensible, arteries stretch in systole (absorbing the pressure wave) and recoil in diastole (pushing blood on). The result is smooth, continuous flow in small vessels.
  • Veins are more distensible than arteries: they have a thinner muscular wall, and arteries have thick smooth muscle (tunica media) and a stiffer structure. Veins are like thin balloons; arteries are like reinforced hoses.
  • Veins hold the most blood: about 60–70% of total blood volume (64% in the Hemodynamics I figure). They are the blood reservoir.

2. Distensibility vs compliance

Distensibility = ΔV / (ΔP × original V)  ·  Compliance = ΔV / ΔP = Distensibility × Volume
  • Distensibility is the fractional stretch per unit pressure, a measure of elasticity.
  • Compliance (capacitance) is the actual volume a vessel takes up per unit pressure, a measure of capacity.
  • Veins are about 8× more distensible than arteries and have about 3× the volume, so venous compliance is about 8 × 3 = 24× arterial compliance. For the same small pressure rise, a vein accepts 24× more blood.

3. Why arteries are high-pressure and veins low-pressure

  • Arteries: right next to the powerful left ventricle; thick walls built to withstand and maintain high pressure; their recoil propels blood; high pressure is needed to drive blood through the high-resistance capillary network.
  • Veins: blood has lost most of its pressure energy crossing the capillaries; high distensibility means extra volume barely raises pressure (the big volume "dilutes" the pressure).

4. Veins as an active reservoir: hemorrhage

Veins can constrict. When blood is lost, sympathetic nerves trigger venoconstriction. The compliant veins shrink and push their stored blood toward the heart, a kind of "self-transfusion" that maintains venous return, cardiac output and blood pressure. The same mechanism boosts cardiac output during exercise.

5. Arterial pressure pulsations

  • Systolic (about 120 mmHg) is the peak, when the LV ejects and stretches the arteries.
  • Diastolic (about 80 mmHg) is the minimum, maintained by the elastic recoil of the arteries.
  • Pulse pressure = systolic − diastolic ≈ 40 mmHg.
  • Two main factors set pulse pressure: (1) stroke volume (bigger SV, more stretch, wider pulse pressure) and (2) arterial compliance (stiffer arteries, wider pulse pressure).
  • The pressure pulse travels along the arteries as a wave. In young, distensible arteries it travels slowly because the walls absorb energy. In stiff, older arteries (arteriosclerosis) it travels faster and is less damped (a "bounding" pulse).

Aging (25-year-old vs 60-year-old)

The 60-year-old usually has higher systolic pressure and a wider pulse pressure. Arteriosclerosis ("hardening") means elastin breaks down and is replaced by stiffer collagen. Distensibility falls, the arteries can't absorb each stroke volume, and systolic pressure rises sharply.

6. Measuring BP: the auscultatory method

  1. Inflate the cuff above systolic pressure. The brachial artery collapses: no flow, no sound.
  2. Slowly deflate.
  3. When cuff pressure falls just below systolic, jets of blood spurt through with each beat. The turbulent flow makes tapping sounds (Korotkoff sounds). The first sound = systolic pressure.
  4. Sounds continue while cuff pressure is between systolic and diastolic (the artery is partly collapsed for part of each cycle).
  5. When cuff pressure falls below diastolic, the artery stays open, flow becomes laminar, and the sounds disappear. That reading = diastolic pressure.

7. Gravity, the venous pump and varicose veins

  • Standing still: the column of blood creates about +90 mmHg venous pressure in the feet.
  • Walking: foot venous pressure is only about +20 mmHg, thanks to the venous (muscle) pump. Contracting leg muscles squeeze the deep veins, and one-way valves let blood go only toward the heart.
  • Standing still for a long time: no pump, so pressure rises to about 90 mmHg and is transmitted back to the capillaries. Hydrostatic pressure far exceeds oncotic pressure, fluid filters out, and the legs swell (edema). Circulating blood volume also falls.
  • Varicose veins: weeks to months of high venous pressure (prolonged standing, pregnancy) overstretch the veins. The veins enlarge but the valves don't, so they become incompetent (leaky). Blood is pushed backward as well as up, pools in superficial veins, raises capillary pressure further, and causes chronic leg edema. It is a vicious cycle.

Lecture 6

Microcirculation

The microcirculation is where the circulation actually does its job: delivering nutrients and O₂ to tissues and removing CO₂ and cell wastes. This lecture covers who controls flow into a capillary bed, how capillaries differ between organs, how substances cross (diffusion), and the four Starling forces that decide whether fluid leaves or re-enters a capillary. The Starling force tables are very likely MCQ material.

1. Scale

  • About 10 billion capillaries in the peripheral circulation.
  • Any cell is only 20–30 µm from a capillary.

2. What controls arteriole diameter?

Increased metabolic activity of the tissue · blood flow · blood pressure · the sympathetic nervous system · hormones. Arterioles are the gatekeepers: working tissue with low O₂ and high CO₂ signals them to dilate.

3. Metarterioles and precapillary sphincters

  • Arterioles have a continuous tunica media, a complete tube of smooth muscle. Constricting it narrows the whole vessel and controls flow to a whole region (broad control).
  • A metarteriole is a short microvessel linking an arteriole to the capillaries (it acts as a thoroughfare). Instead of a continuous muscle coat it has scattered individual smooth muscle cells.
  • Where each true capillary branches off, a ring of smooth muscle forms a precapillary sphincter that opens or closes that capillary's entrance.
  • Most important: metarterioles and precapillary sphincters are in close contact with the tissues they serve, so local metabolic conditions control them directly. This is hyper-local control of flow within a tissue.

4. Artery vs vein structure (microcirculation slide)

  • Both have endothelium, smooth muscle and connective tissue layers.
  • Arteries have a thicker smooth-muscle layer to withstand high pressure and the pulse.
  • Veins have valves (one-way flow, important in the legs against gravity) and more space/volume. Less muscle makes them more distensible, so they act as reservoirs.

5. Three types of capillary

Blood–brain barrier (BBB)

  • Brain capillary endothelial cells are joined by tight junctions, so only very small molecules (water, O₂, CO₂) pass freely and toxins are kept out.
  • The barrier is reinforced by the basement membrane, pericytes and astrocyte end-feet. Brain endothelial cells are also rich in mitochondria (active transport).
  • How nutrients get into the brain: diffusion (small, lipid-soluble molecules and gases) · limited paracellular transport · transport proteins (carriers, e.g. for glucose) · receptor-mediated transcytosis · adsorptive transcytosis. Efflux pumps push unwanted molecules back out.

6. Diffusion: the main way substances cross

  • Substances move between plasma and interstitial fluid mainly by diffusion: random thermal motion, back and forth through the wall, which keeps plasma and interstitial fluid continually mixed.
  • The net rate of diffusion is proportional to the concentration difference across the membrane. A bigger difference gives faster net movement.
  • O₂: higher in capillary blood than in interstitial fluid (especially at the arterial end), so it moves blood → tissues.
  • CO₂: higher in tissues (cells produce it), so it moves tissues → blood (picked up toward the venous end).
  • Even slight concentration differences give more than adequate transport.

7. Interstitial fluid

  • Interstitial fluid (tissue fluid) is the fluid in the spaces around cells. It forms mainly from what leaks out of capillaries. It brings O₂ and nutrients to cells and takes away wastes. As new fluid forms, older fluid drains into lymph vessels, where it is called lymph.
  • It contains water, minerals, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, hormones and a small amount of protein.
  • Most of it is trapped in a gel of proteoglycan filaments. Only tiny rivulets and vesicles of free fluid flow.
  • In edema, the pockets of free fluid expand tremendously until half or more of the edema fluid is free-flowing.

10. Applying it: what causes edema?

Anything that raises outward forces or lowers inward forces increases filtration:

  • ↑ Capillary pressure (venous obstruction, standing still, heart failure)
  • ↓ Plasma proteins and Πp (liver failure, since the liver makes albumin; protein loss in the kidney; malnutrition)
  • ↑ Capillary permeability, so protein leaks and Πif rises (inflammation, histamine)
  • Blocked lymphatics: the normal excess filtrate and protein can't be returned (next lecture)

Lecture 7

Lymphatic System

Microcirculation left us with a small daily surplus of filtered fluid and leaked protein in the tissues. The lymphatic system is the overflow drain that returns it. It also has two other jobs: absorbing dietary fat and running immune surveillance. Know the three functions, why lymphatic capillaries can take up protein, the lymph node layout, the germinal center steps, and why fat goes into lymph instead of blood.

1. What the lymphatic system is and does

A network of vessels, lymph nodes and organs that runs parallel to the circulatory system and:

  1. Collects excess interstitial fluid from tissues and returns it to the bloodstream.
  2. Absorbs dietary fats from the intestine.
  3. Contributes to immune defense.

The problem it solves: the slight net filtration leaves roughly 2–3 L/day of fluid (about 10% of what is filtered) plus leaked plasma proteins in the tissues. Proteins cannot re-enter blood capillaries on their own. Without lymphatic return of protein and fluid, massive edema develops and death follows within about 24 hours.

2. Lymphoid organs and tissues

OrganRole
ThymusSite of T-cell maturation

Lymph nodesFilter lymph. Hundreds, linked by lymph vessels, clustered at the neck (cervical), armpit (axillary), groin (inguinal) and abdomen

3. The vessels: from capillary to vein

Interstitial fluid → lymphatic capillary (now "lymph") → collecting lymphatic vessels (with valves) → lymph nodes → thoracic duct (via the cisterna chyli) or right lymphatic duct → subclavian veins near the heart
  • Lymphatic capillaries are blind-ended tubes woven through the tissues. Their endothelial cells overlap like flaps that act as one-way valves: fluid pushes the flaps open to enter, but can't flow back out.
  • Anchoring filaments tie the endothelial cells to surrounding connective tissue. When tissue swells, they pull the flaps open.
  • This special structure lets high-molecular-weight substances (proteins, large particles, bacteria, chylomicrons) enter lymph, which ordinary blood capillaries can't take back up.
  • Larger lymph vessels have smooth muscle and one-way valves, so lymph moves only toward the heart.
  • Lymph flows only in lymphatic vessels; it does not pass through arteries or veins until it is emptied into the venous system near the heart.
  • The thoracic duct drains most of the body (and carries the chylomicrons from the gut). The right lymphatic duct drains the right upper body.

4. Lymph: what is in it

Interstitial fluid that wasn't reabsorbed by capillaries: water, electrolytes, proteins, fats/chylomicrons and fat-soluble vitamins (from the gut), wastes and cell debris, damaged or cancer cells, pathogens and antigens, and lymphocytes and other WBCs. It is a clear fluid.

5. Effects of lymphatic drainage (slide: three effects)

  1. Prevents fluid accumulation (edema). If fluid enters tissues faster than it is removed, interstitial volume rises and causes swelling.
  2. Maintains normal interstitial fluid pressure by continuously removing excess fluid, so tissue pressure doesn't climb.
  3. Maintains blood volume and plasma protein concentration. Returning fluid and protein keeps the circulating volume and the plasma proteins that create the osmotic pull drawing water into blood.

Other roles from the notes: removing proteins and large particles from the interstitium; transporting chylomicrons; regulating inflammation by filtering pathogens; a backup drain when circulation is impaired (e.g. heart failure).

6. When lymphatics fail

  • Edema, localized or generalized. Long-standing blockage gives lymphedema, with tissue thickening and hardening.
  • Raised tissue pressure, causing discomfort and limited mobility.
  • Impaired delivery of nutrients and O₂ (longer diffusion distance through excess fluid).
  • Higher infection risk from compromised immune surveillance.

8. The germinal center (the antibody "R&D department")

It forms in secondary follicles only during an active immune response. Residents: B cells (the workers), follicular dendritic cells (FDCs), the "librarians" that display intact antigen and don't destroy it, and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, the "supervisors" that give survival signals.

  1. Proliferation (clonal expansion): an activated B cell divides rapidly into thousands of identical clones.
  2. Somatic hypermutation: deliberate random mutations in the antibody (immunoglobulin) genes for the antigen-binding site, creating variants.
  3. Affinity maturation and selection: B cells compete to bind antigen on FDCs. High-affinity cells get Tfh survival signals; low-affinity cells undergo apoptosis and are cleared by macrophages.
  4. Differentiation: winners become plasma cells (go to the medulla, pump out antibodies) or memory B cells (long-lived, give a faster, stronger response next time).

Why it matters: high-affinity antibodies neutralize antigens effectively, and good vaccines aim to trigger strong germinal center responses.

It is a sign the immune system is working, like a military base on high alert.

10. Fat absorption goes through the lymph

  1. Emulsification by bile: bile salts break large fat droplets into small ones (more surface area).
  2. Digestion by pancreatic lipase: triglycerides → monoglycerides + free fatty acids.
  3. Micelles (bile salts and lecithin with a hydrophilic outer shell and a hydrophobic core) ferry fatty acids, monoglycerides and cholesterol to the absorptive cells of the villi.
  4. Inside the epithelial cell, fatty acids and monoglycerides are re-joined into triglycerides, then combined with proteins in the Golgi to form chylomicrons: a triglyceride core inside a shell of phospholipid, cholesterol and protein.
  5. Chylomicrons are extruded (exocytosis) and enter the lacteal, the special lymphatic capillary in each villus.
  6. Lymph carries them through lymph vessels to the thoracic duct, and they enter the blood near the heart.

Size rule: short- and medium-chain fatty acids and glycerol (small) go straight into blood capillaries. Long-chain fatty acids and monoglycerides are made into triglycerides and chylomicrons, which go into lymph. Glucose and amino acids go directly into blood.

Why don't chylomicrons go straight into blood?

  1. Fats don't dissolve in water, so they have to be packaged into lipoprotein particles (chylomicrons) for transport in watery blood or lymph.
  2. Chylomicrons are large particles. They enter the more permeable lymphatic capillaries (lacteals) of the villi far more easily than ordinary blood capillaries.

Bonus from the notes: entry by lymph gives a controlled, gradual release into the blood at the thoracic duct, which protects against a sudden lipid overload.

11. Formation of interstitial fluid (recap)

Interstitial fluid is part of the extracellular fluid. It is formed from plasma forced out of capillaries by hydrostatic pressure generated in systole: water, ions and small solutes pass through the gaps between endothelial cells. It also collects metabolic wastes from the cells.
